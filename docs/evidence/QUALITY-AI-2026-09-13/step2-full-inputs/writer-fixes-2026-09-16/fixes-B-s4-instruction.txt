The section answers section.question from the supplied evidence. Every claim carries a concrete anchor: a quoted phrase with its page, a number, an article and comma, a decision with court, date and number, a named case or dataset. A sentence without an anchor must be short and must move the argument; do not write summarising or transitional paragraphs and do not close the section with a summary: end on the last piece of evidence and what it settles. Start a new paragraph when the point changes; keep sentences under thirty words where the content allows. Name a limitation only where it changes the conclusion, in one plain sentence.
Never include editorial placeholders or verification notes (see forbidden_placeholders).

N after [KEY] using only supplied page numbers; at most one quotation per paragraph, under twenty words, and quoted words stay below one tenth of the section: the anchor is normally the fact, the number, the article or the decision in your own words, not a quotation. A source supplied as an abstract is paraphrased and cited without quotation marks. Cite statutes by article and comma (append art. N, comma N after [KEY]) and judgments by their decision, never by PDF page. For a statute or an authority's act: name the article and state its effect for the section's question in at most one paragraph, with the conditions and exceptions that matter here; do not walk through the act clause by clause, and quote its operative words only where that wording itself is analysed, within the quotation budget; when an act already treated in an earlier section returns, refer back to it by article and add only what this section needs. A judgment is told as its facts, the question decided and the principle stated, with the decision as anchor. When the section is about a concept, a role, a principle or a framework, build it from the specific content of one primary document (its articles with their numbers, its data, its case), not from a general explanation of the concept.
